# Meaningful Builder Campaign — hands-on chapter blueprint

**Status:** authored design draft; not implemented chapters.  
**Date:** 1 November 2026.  
**Direction:** PipuPath develops people who discover needs and create useful change. Construction is one medium among tools, services, learning, art, enterprise, coordination and stewardship.

## Campaign contract

- The city is a living community and a visible record of consequences. Existing structures can host meaningful challenges without another building being added.
- Core rhythm: notice a need → make or organise a response → let it operate → inspect consequences → improve and hand over.
- Each chapter has three different missions: 36 authored missions overall. Never disguise the same carry-kit/build/open sequence as every kind of work.
- Missions happen through the character, objects, equipment and residents in the world. Brief conversations and compact controls support action; answer cards and detached puzzle boards are not the main challenge.
- A mission passes on a demonstrated useful outcome. Completing a prescribed number of taps, moving supplies or revealing a polished model is insufficient.
- Several workable approaches should exist wherever the simulated problem permits them. Failure reveals a specific causal problem and leaves a recoverable next move.
- Stakes increase through competing needs, dependencies, changing demand and handover. Difficulty must not rely on unclear instructions, tiny targets, arbitrary waits or repetitive fetching.
- Human benefit, reliability, responsible resource use and inclusion guide feedback. Wealth, skyline height and building count do not define builder success.
- Fictional city progress remains separate from real-world evidence, verified capability and existing XP rules. Do not infer a real person's abilities solely from game choices.

## Mission design cards

Each numbered action is a quest step. The final column supplies a short reflection and a clear next action. These are design specifications; each outcome needs an actual simulation/interaction implementation before it can be called playable.

## Chapter 1 — Notice What Matters

**Builder habit:** Observe before deciding what to fix.

### 1.1 Follow the missing water

1. Walk to the waiting households.
2. Ask a resident to show their route.
3. Follow the supply to its source.
4. Inspect the interrupted service.
5. Run a small restoration test.

**Evidence of completion:** The source of the interruption is demonstrated by the service changing, not by selecting a diagnosis.

**Reflection:** What did you observe that changed your first explanation?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 1.2.

### 1.2 A route for everyone

1. Meet a resident using a mobility aid.
2. Accompany their journey.
3. Inspect the inaccessible crossing.
4. Rearrange the obstruction and access ramp.
5. Watch the resident repeat the journey.

**Evidence of completion:** The resident reaches the destination without the player carrying them or blocking another route.

**Reflection:** Who could use this place before your change, and who can use it now?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 1.3.

### 1.3 Where the food disappears

1. Visit a grower.
2. Follow one harvest shipment.
3. Inspect its handling and storage.
4. Change one suspected source of loss.
5. Compare the next shipment.

**Evidence of completion:** The changed handling demonstrably reduces loss; unused guesses earn no completion.

**Reflection:** Which evidence separated the symptom from its cause?

**Next action:** Enter Chapter 2 and apply this habit to a different kind of need.

## Chapter 2 — Make Things Work Again

**Builder habit:** Diagnose, repair and reuse instead of replacing everything.

### 2.1 The repair workshop

1. Bring a damaged tool to the bench.
2. Inspect its moving parts.
3. Isolate it safely with the workshop supervisor.
4. Replace the verified failed component.
5. Operate it under load.

**Evidence of completion:** A fictional supervised load test succeeds; cosmetic replacement alone fails.

**Reflection:** What could be kept rather than thrown away?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 2.2.

### 2.2 Reopen the market

1. Inspect the traders’ damaged stalls.
2. Collect and fit useful repair materials.
3. Move the obstructing cart.
4. Protect the produce in storage.
5. Open the market and inspect its operation.

**Evidence of completion:** Shelter, access and protected stock all work; the existing market prototype supplies the interaction foundation.

**Reflection:** Which repair helped people most?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 2.3.

### 2.3 Reuse what remains

1. Collect discarded components.
2. Inspect which are usable.
3. Assemble a useful carrying aid.
4. Test it with a resident’s load.
5. Improve the part that fails.

**Evidence of completion:** The reused object carries its intended load and can be operated by the resident.

**Reflection:** What changed waste into something useful?

**Next action:** Enter Chapter 3 and apply this habit to a different kind of need.

## Chapter 3 — Grow and Share Enough

**Builder habit:** Understand food as a connected system of soil, production, storage and access.

### 3.1 Keep the soil alive

1. Inspect contrasting beds.
2. Select and plant a suitable mix.
3. Adjust mulch and water in the actual plots.
4. Run a weather cycle.
5. Improve the stressed bed.

**Evidence of completion:** Crops survive the cycle within the water limit; one oversized crop does not hide another bed failing.

**Reflection:** What did the soil need, beyond simply planting more?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 3.2.

### 3.2 Harvest without waste

1. Inspect ripe crops.
2. Organise harvesting containers.
3. Choose sheltered collection points.
4. Move produce into suitable storage.
5. Observe the stored harvest through a hot period.

**Evidence of completion:** Useful food retained increases; storage labels alone do not count.

**Reflection:** Where did the greatest avoidable loss happen?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 3.3.

### 3.3 Get meals to households

1. Visit households with different needs.
2. Collect available ingredients.
3. Prepare and portion meals at a supervised station.
4. Organise delivery.
5. Inspect who received sufficient food.

**Evidence of completion:** The simulation shows households served and unmet needs, rather than counting packages moved.

**Reflection:** Who would have been missed by an equal but unsuitable allocation?

**Next action:** Enter Chapter 4 and apply this habit to a different kind of need.

## Chapter 4 — Care for People

**Builder habit:** Build wellbeing through access, organisation and practical support.

### 4.1 A clinic that can receive people

1. Observe arrivals at an existing clinic.
2. Speak with the fictional trained clinician.
3. Arrange reception and waiting flow.
4. Assign support staff and supplies.
5. Run a busy arrival period.

**Evidence of completion:** People reach the appropriate trained staff with safe access and reduced waiting; the player does not practise medical diagnosis.

**Reflection:** What was making care harder to reach?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 4.2.

### 4.2 Clean water where it is needed

1. Inspect existing wash points.
2. Collect cleaning and refill supplies.
3. Restore a station with the responsible operator.
4. Position supplies where people can reach them.
5. Observe use and refill needs.

**Evidence of completion:** Stations remain usable during simulated demand instead of merely looking clean.

**Reflection:** What keeps a useful service working after its opening?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 4.3.

### 4.3 A safer hot day

1. Follow the journeys of exposed residents.
2. Identify places to rest.
3. Organise shade and drinking-water access.
4. Guide people toward the safe route.
5. Run the heat period.

**Evidence of completion:** Residents reach rest and water without closing essential access.

**Reflection:** Which people faced the highest burden?

**Next action:** Enter Chapter 5 and apply this habit to a different kind of need.

## Chapter 5 — Help Others Learn

**Builder habit:** A builder can create another person’s opportunity to succeed.

### 5.1 Make a learning corner

1. Ask a learner what they are trying to do.
2. Arrange existing materials for that task.
3. Demonstrate one useful technique.
4. Let the learner try without assistance.
5. Change the support after observing the attempt.

**Evidence of completion:** The learner completes a new attempt independently; furnishing a room alone is insufficient.

**Reflection:** What did the learner do that they could not do before?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 5.2.

### 5.2 An apprenticeship that works

1. Observe a workshop task.
2. Divide it into safe practice steps.
3. Prepare a practice station with the supervisor.
4. Guide an apprentice’s first attempt.
5. Let the apprentice perform the next attempt alone.

**Evidence of completion:** The apprentice completes the fictional task safely without the player substituting their own work.

**Reflection:** When did helping become doing the task for someone?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 5.3.

### 5.3 Reach the learners left out

1. Visit learners missing the session.
2. Investigate practical access barriers.
3. Change the time, route or shared resource.
4. Run the revised session.
5. Inspect attendance and independent attempts.

**Evidence of completion:** Previously excluded learners can participate without displacing another group.

**Reflection:** Which assumption about access proved wrong?

**Next action:** Enter Chapter 6 and apply this habit to a different kind of need.

## Chapter 6 — Invent Useful Tools and Places

**Builder habit:** Turn a need into a prototype, then a dependable tool or space.

### 6.1 A useful prototype

1. Observe a resident struggling with a task.
2. Assemble a simple alternative at the workshop.
3. Let the resident use it.
4. Measure the failed part of the attempt.
5. Revise and retest.

**Evidence of completion:** The intended user completes the task more successfully; assembling parts alone is not success.

**Reflection:** What did user testing teach you that your first design missed?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 6.2.

### 6.2 Program a helpful response

1. Inspect a recurring service failure.
2. Place a sensor on the real city equipment.
3. Set a condition and response on its controller.
4. Run normal and fault scenarios.
5. Correct false alarms and missed alerts.

**Evidence of completion:** The equipment responds correctly in both scenarios; there is no detached connector puzzle.

**Reflection:** When should a system act, and when should it ask for help?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 6.3.

### 6.3 Build a place that serves

1. Choose a demonstrated need and an appropriate reserved plot.
2. Bring the site materials.
3. Assemble the essential structure.
4. Connect access and required services.
5. Observe its intended use under demand.

**Evidence of completion:** Homes, hospital, farm, stadium, tower or airport are contextual choices; opening must prove a purpose, not merely reveal a model.

**Reflection:** What useful activity does the place make possible?

**Next action:** Enter Chapter 7 and apply this habit to a different kind of need.

## Chapter 7 — Create Culture and Understanding

**Builder habit:** Make art, communication and shared experiences useful to people.

### 7.1 Help people find their way

1. Accompany a first-time visitor.
2. Identify confusing decision points.
3. Place symbols and directions in the city.
4. Watch a new visitor travel without guidance.
5. Revise the confusing sign.

**Evidence of completion:** A fresh visitor reaches the destination independently; decorative signs alone do not count.

**Reflection:** What was obvious to you but unclear to someone else?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 7.2.

### 7.2 A performance that brings people together

1. Listen to residents’ ideas.
2. Assemble an accessible stage area using existing resources.
3. Arrange performers and sound.
4. Rehearse the experience with an audience.
5. Run and improve the event.

**Evidence of completion:** People can attend, hear and participate without unsafe crowding or blocked access.

**Reflection:** Whose contribution changed the event?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 7.3.

### 7.3 Tell a useful community story

1. Find a resident-led issue.
2. Collect consented fictional accounts.
3. Arrange the accounts and visuals into a coherent message.
4. Show it to a different resident.
5. Revise what was misunderstood.

**Evidence of completion:** The audience can act on an accurate message; collecting recordings is not the outcome.

**Reflection:** What did you change to communicate faithfully?

**Next action:** Enter Chapter 8 and apply this habit to a different kind of need.

## Chapter 8 — Create Value People Need

**Builder habit:** Enterprise starts with a useful response to a real need.

### 8.1 The first useful service

1. Visit potential fictional customers.
2. Observe an unmet task.
3. Organise a small service using existing resources.
4. Deliver it to one customer.
5. Improve it from their response.

**Evidence of completion:** The customer’s problem is resolved; revenue is fictional and never a promise of real-world income.

**Reflection:** What made the service useful enough to request?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 8.2.

### 8.2 Deliver what was promised

1. Inspect a set of orders.
2. Organise available stock and workstations.
3. Schedule the work to available capacity.
4. Fulfil the orders.
5. Inspect quality and missed commitments.

**Evidence of completion:** Orders are fulfilled to their requirements within resource limits, not simply marked complete.

**Reflection:** Where did promising too much reduce trust?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 8.3.

### 8.3 Keep the service sustainable

1. Inspect the service’s resource flow.
2. Identify a recurring waste.
3. Change sourcing or work sequence.
4. Run another operating cycle.
5. Compare usefulness, cost and fairness.

**Evidence of completion:** The service continues within its fictional resources while meeting agreed needs.

**Reflection:** Which shortcut would have helped the numbers but harmed people?

**Next action:** Enter Chapter 9 and apply this habit to a different kind of need.

## Chapter 9 — Connect the City

**Builder habit:** Coordinate existing services so that people can rely on them.

### 9.1 A delivery network that reaches people

1. Inspect destination needs.
2. Load suitable goods into existing vehicles.
3. Assign routes and delivery windows.
4. Run deliveries.
5. Repair the missed or congested route.

**Evidence of completion:** Required goods arrive at the intended places; empty vehicle movement earns no progress.

**Reflection:** Which connection was more valuable than another building?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 9.2.

### 9.2 Keep essential power working

1. Inspect actual demand during an outage.
2. Isolate the faulty branch with the fictional utility operator.
3. Dispatch a repair crew.
4. Manage temporary supply for essential services.
5. Restore and test normal operation.

**Evidence of completion:** The essential services remain supported and the fault is repaired, without pretending supply is unlimited.

**Reflection:** How did you choose what needed support first?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 9.3.

### 9.3 A journey people can complete

1. Follow several passenger journeys.
2. Place stops and organise existing vehicles.
3. Make transfers and crossings usable.
4. Run peak demand.
5. Improve the stranded passengers’ route.

**Evidence of completion:** People complete useful journeys safely rather than merely increasing vehicle speed.

**Reflection:** Who was still stranded when the average journey improved?

**Next action:** Enter Chapter 10 and apply this habit to a different kind of need.

## Chapter 10 — Build Trust and Teams

**Builder habit:** Create coordination that succeeds beyond the player’s individual effort.

### 10.1 Organise a shared mission

1. Observe a community job.
2. Meet fictional volunteers and their capabilities.
3. Assign complementary responsibilities.
4. Let the team attempt the job.
5. Revise the handoffs that fail.

**Evidence of completion:** The team completes its work without the player performing every task.

**Reflection:** What needed clearer ownership?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 10.2.

### 10.2 Resolve competing needs

1. Visit two groups sharing a limited resource.
2. Observe when and why each needs it.
3. Arrange a workable schedule at the shared facility.
4. Let both groups try it.
5. Revise from observed consequences.

**Evidence of completion:** Both groups can perform essential activities within the actual resource constraint.

**Reflection:** What made an agreement workable rather than merely agreeable?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 10.3.

### 10.3 Leave a reliable handover

1. Observe recurring breakdowns.
2. Identify a responsible team.
3. Place an operating routine and escalation rule on its work board.
4. Leave the site while the team runs it.
5. Return to inspect the result.

**Evidence of completion:** The team handles normal work and raises the defined exception without requiring the player’s continuous presence.

**Reflection:** What should be delegated, and what should trigger help?

**Next action:** Enter Chapter 11 and apply this habit to a different kind of need.

## Chapter 11 — Protect Tomorrow

**Builder habit:** Care for shared resources and prepare for disruption.

### 11.1 Close the waste loop

1. Follow discarded materials through the city.
2. Organise practical sorting and collection.
3. Direct reusable items to a repair or production site.
4. Run the collection cycle.
5. Inspect remaining waste and contamination.

**Evidence of completion:** Useful material re-enters production and streets remain clear; sorting animations alone are insufficient.

**Reflection:** What made reuse practical for the next person?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 11.2.

### 11.2 Prepare before the storm

1. Inspect exposed routes and services.
2. Clear obstructions and prepare existing shelters.
3. Assign crews and accessible evacuation routes.
4. Run the storm scenario.
5. Improve the locations still at risk.

**Evidence of completion:** Residents reach safety and essential services recover; the action remains directly in the city.

**Reflection:** Which preparation reduced harm most?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 11.3.

### 11.3 Restore a living place

1. Inspect a degraded green space.
2. Select suitable planting and protection measures.
3. Organise water and maintenance responsibility.
4. Run dry and wet periods.
5. Revise the failing section.

**Evidence of completion:** The space remains usable and its planted areas survive within maintenance limits.

**Reflection:** Who will care for this place when the first planting is over?

**Next action:** Enter Chapter 12 and apply this habit to a different kind of need.

## Chapter 12 — Leave Something That Lasts

**Builder habit:** Combine useful creation, evidence, stewardship and real-world action.

### 12.1 Your meaningful contribution

1. Revisit an unresolved resident need.
2. Choose a tool, service, place or shared experience.
3. Organise and make the solution.
4. Test it with its intended users.
5. Improve the weakest observed outcome.

**Evidence of completion:** The player demonstrates a useful outcome using mechanics learned earlier; an expensive landmark is not automatically better.

**Reflection:** What changed for another person because you acted?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 12.2.

### 12.2 The city runs without you

1. Inspect dependencies in the player’s contribution.
2. Assign operators and maintenance resources.
3. Define a response to predictable faults.
4. Run several cycles with new pressures.
5. Inspect whether the contribution still works.

**Evidence of completion:** The contribution withstands normal demand and a simulated disruption without continuous player intervention.

**Reflection:** What would cause this to fail after you leave?

**Next action:** Continue to mission 12.3.

### 12.3 Take the builder into the world

1. Choose an achievable local need inspired by play.
2. Prepare a small real-world experiment.
3. Act within relevant permissions and safety boundaries.
4. Submit honest evidence of the attempt.
5. Reflect and choose a next action.

**Evidence of completion:** This uses the protected Real Quest journey: no simulated city event is presented as real-world evidence or verified capability.

**Reflection:** What actually happened, and what will you change next?

**Next action:** Continue the Real Quest from its actual result, including an honest failed attempt.

## Build once, replicate through a city team

A successfully tested prototype unlocks a reusable standard for its building family. The player is not asked to repeat construction on every plot. A crew can reproduce approved variations on suitable, preassigned plots as later chapters establish the needed people, resources, access and operating systems.

The core sequence is **make one → prove it works → establish the standard → assign a crew → replicate where useful → maintain the result**. Replication is visible fictional city work, not a claim of real-world development. The model must retain which prototype, plots, crew and resources produced each instance. Reopening a celebration or chapter must not duplicate buildings or consume resources twice.

| Prototype family    | What replication should produce                                                                           | What limits it                                                                                   |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Homes               | Several orderly neighbourhood clusters, with varied façades and rooflines                                 | Household needs, utilities, pedestrian access and green space                                    |
| Tower               | A coherent skyline of approved height and façade variations                                               | Suitable plots, foundation limits, access, power and useful demand                               |
| Hospital            | A sensible network of smaller clinics and a central hospital                                              | Catchment needs, trained teams, supplies, water and access                                       |
| Farm                | Crop plots, growing zones and storage serving the city                                                    | Land use, water, soil health, storage and distribution                                           |
| Stadium             | A main venue plus appropriate local sports grounds                                                        | Community access, open space and operating needs                                                 |
| Airport             | One planned airfield growing through terminal, hangar and logistics improvements                          | Reserved land, safety spacing and network demand; do not clone airports into neighbourhood plots |
| Other useful places | Schools, workshops, markets, cultural spaces and utilities from validated or already-authorised templates | The specific demonstrated need and a suitable operating model                                    |

“As many as possible” is not an unlimited-spawn rule. A flourishing city has a balanced land-use plan. More copies are desirable only where they add useful capacity or a coherent built environment; they must not consume every park, block routes or imply that empty facilities are functioning.

A first player prototype may unlock approved variants of its family. Other building families still need their own validated template: either a demonstrated player build or an explicitly authored, tested city-team standard. Organising clinic services can unlock deployment of that approved clinic standard without requiring another construction minigame. The game must explain the team's contribution instead of crediting the player with work they did not perform.

## Twelve-chapter growth and final city

The existing layout currently exposes **70 developable parcels**, in addition to reserved district worksites. The pacing below is a proposed design target for those parcels, not a claim of implemented replication or live population. “Developed” includes purposeful green, civic and public spaces; it does not mean paving the entire world or representing every small parcel as a full-scale airport.

| 3. Grow and Share Enough            | Food production, protection and access         | Growing zones, storage and distribution places develop                                                     |                       10 |
| 4. Care for People                  | Working care access and support services       | Approved clinics, wash points and accessible paths expand                                                  |                       15 |

| 8. Create Value People Need         | Dependable services and useful enterprise      | Markets, service premises and productive workshops expand                                                  |                       41 |

Targets must be checked against the actual spatial plan and simulation before implementation. A mission's visible response may include occupancy, repairs, people using existing buildings, public art, crop health, lighting or operating activity rather than a new structure every time. One completed mission can enable a coordinated batch of city development; there is no requirement to perform 70 separate builds.

The final city should have a recognisable skyline, populated neighbourhoods, working care/learning/food/enterprise systems, connected transport, useful landmarks and deliberate open spaces. Cosmetic finishing standardises alignment, façade families, footpaths, entrances, planting and signage. Service failures remain visible until resolved; beautification cannot silently turn a failed test into a working facility.

Each chapter has one deliberate celebration: show what changed for people, reveal the city-team contribution, then offer the next meaningful challenge. Chapter 12 celebrates a thriving system and a real-world next step, not simply the maximum number of structures. The final city must continue to offer operation, improvement and Real Quests rather than end at an unexplained dead end.

## Reusable systems and authoring ownership

A shared interaction engine supplies observation, dialogue, carrying, repair, operation, arrangement, scheduling, instruction/demonstration, prototyping, testing and delegation. Each mission supplies its own causal rules, resource limits, resident needs, success evidence, failure feedback and next action. Shared controls must not force every mission into one mechanic.

The mission author owns the design card and initial scenario. The simulation developer owns the outcome model and scene interactions. A reviewer owns causal/interaction checks. A playtest lead owns unassisted comprehension and player feedback. Routine mission acceptance uses these roles and evidence; the founder is not the default approver for every mission.

The completed city uses curated visual finishing: entrances, paths, signs, furniture, crops and structures settle into readable, accessible layouts. Finishing can tidy appearance but must not erase a poor functional decision or pretend an unmet need was resolved.

## Implementation order

1. Preserve the working construction/market prototypes as reusable interaction sources; do not relabel them as the entire new campaign.
2. Build one short episode with three genuinely different missions: investigate a service interruption (1.1), organise clinic flow (4.1), and help an apprentice succeed independently (5.2). These prove observation, service organisation and teaching beyond construction.
3. Test whether players understand the need, act without an adult explaining, interpret failure and voluntarily improve their response. Record confusion, time to first meaningful action, recovery and outcome quality; do not manufacture confidence or engagement metrics.
4. Only then migrate the ordered campaign, its saves, celebration gates and Real Quest context. Specify old-save migration before replacing existing chapter progress.
5. Expand chapter-by-chapter with at least two viable solution strategies where applicable, accessible equivalents, performance checks and whole-journey regression coverage.

## Existing product boundary

The current preview contains market repair and six construction worksites. The existing twelve story chapters still use their previous campaign progression. None of the 36 missions above is claimed to be playable merely because this document exists. Existing useful operations, simulation rules and authored situations should be reused where they fit, while their interactions are redesigned.
